A capsule machine has no brew group, no grinder and no valve unit, so its faults concentrate in a few overlooked parts. The piercing plate, the capsule sensor and the drip-tray switch account for most of the "machine is possessed" reports in the [Nespresso section](https://codefixcoffee.com/nespresso/). None requires a new machine; most cases need ten minutes and a soft brush.

## The piercing plate: small part, total authority over your coffee

At the top of every capsule machine, the head clamps the pod against a gasket and a plate punctures the foil so pressurised water can enter. Vertuo models also spin the capsule while brewing, which makes a clean, centred pierce even more important.

Over hundreds of cycles — a two-person household runs well over a thousand capsules a year — the pierce points dull or bend, residue packs into the plate, and the gasket ages. The result mimics every other capsule-machine fault, so the plate is usually checked last.

### Symptoms of a tired piercing plate

- Flow slows or weakens even after a full descale — the classic false lead, since the restriction is a packed plate, not limescale.
- Coffee splutters or spits at the head.
- A capsule ejects heavy and bulging, still full of water — clamped, but never properly opened.
- Grounds appear in the cup.
- Extraction turns noisy or uneven — an off-centre pierce cannot hold pressure.

### Care you can do, care you should not

1. Switch off, unplug, and let the head cool.
2. Remove and rinse the capsule holder — usually dishwasher-safe, though the head never is.
3. Inspect the plate under good light: packed residue, bent points, a cracked gasket.
4. Clean with a soft nylon brush and warm water only — no needles or metal picks, which bend the pierce points you are trying to save.
5. Run a rinse cycle, then test with a fresh genuine capsule.

If the symptoms survive all five steps, the plate itself is the suspect — it sits under spring pressure and is a job for assistance, not user service.

## The capsule sensor that cries wolf

Vertuo machines read the barcode around the capsule rim through a small window in the head; Original-line machines simply detect whether a capsule is present. Both fail in two directions:

- "No capsule" is reported although one is loaded and the lever is closed.
- It tries to brew empty, or throws a capsule fault the moment it pierces.

Nine times out of ten the fix is cleaning: splatter on the window, residue in the holder, or the previous cycle's capsule still jammed so the new one cannot seat. On connected Vertuo models the 1301 family covers this sensor branch — our [1301–1305 reference](https://codefixcoffee.com/nespresso/vertuo-machines/1301-1305/) separates pod-sensor cases from descale cases wearing the same number, and the [blinking-lights guide](https://codefixcoffee.com/nespresso/vertuo-machines/blinking-lights/) decodes the ring first.

## The drip tray and its microswitch

Underneath most drip trays sits a float — often a magnet on a buoy — read by a microswitch. When the tray fills, the float rises and the machine refuses to brew until emptied. The system lies in predictable ways:

- "Empty the drip tray" appears although the tray is empty.
- Brewing starts, then aborts after a few seconds.
- The message clears only when you press or wiggle the tray.

The causes are mundane: a float jammed with sludge or scale, a magnet shaken loose, a tray not clicked home, or wet contacts. Wash the tray, work the float until it moves freely, descale a scale-bound one with diluted citric acid, and reseat until it clicks. It is the same logic as a Philips or Saeco [Error 04](https://codefixcoffee.com/philips-saeco/espresso-machines/error-04/) — a detection switch complaining about seating, not a broken machine.

## The recycling loop that leaks: reused capsules

Aluminium capsules are highly recyclable, and [nespresso.com](https://www.nespresso.com) runs collection schemes that reprocess the metal — the intended loop is melting the aluminium, not a second brew. Reuse happens anyway: refillable pods, re-foiled empties, capsules rinsed and resealed.

The physics problem is the rim: a precision sealing surface. Once dented, aged or re-crimped by hand, the foil no longer presses flat against the head gasket, so water escapes around the rim instead of through the coffee — leaks at the head, drips down the front, puddles in the base. Grounds from hand-filled pods also migrate into the plate and sensor window, accelerating both faults above.

A bean-to-cup machine at least announces this: water in the base trips a leak alarm on models like a Magnifica or Dinamica — the sensor and drying sequence are covered in our [leak alarm code 3963 guide](https://codefixcoffee.com/delonghi/magnifica-dinamica/leak-alarm-code-3963/) and [De'Longhi's documentation](https://www.delonghi.com). Most capsule machines carry no base sensor, so the first sign is water on the counter.

If you use refillable pods — a set runs €10 to €20 — choose ones designed for your system, inspect the rim before every brew, and clean the window and plate more often. A genuinely used capsule belongs in the recycling bag, not back in the head.

## When to stop and call it in

Clean plate, clean sensor window, free-moving float, one fresh genuine capsule — if the fault survives all four, book the service request. And stop early on any leak you cannot tie to a capsule: water and the base electronics do not negotiate.
