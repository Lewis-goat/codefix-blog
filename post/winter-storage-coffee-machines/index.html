Winter and storage: cold-machine errors and how to avoid them

2026-11-01

Every winter the same story arrives with the deliveries. A coffee machine comes off an unheated van, spends a night in a garage, and greets its owner with an error code instead of coffee. Usually nothing is broken. Cold triggers a family of protective lockouts — the machine refuses to heat until it has warmed up — and the codes look identical to real sensor failures. Warming the machine correctly is the whole fix; this guide covers the codes, the condensation risk, and a safe warm-up protocol.

The cold-lockout codes

  • Jura Error 2 is the most common Jura code and has two faces: below roughly 10 °C the heater is locked out until the machine warms, or the coffee thermoblock's sensor circuit has gone open. The warming protocol on the Jura Error 2 page separates the two.
  • Jura Errors 1 and 5 can also be tripped by a machine that has just come out of a cold car or garage. Error 5, a heating failure, lists a very cold machine as a cause to rule out first.
  • Philips and Saeco Error 11 or 19 is explicitly the temperature version: the internal sensors read below the minimum operating temperature after winter transport or garage storage, and the machine locks the heater. Philips' documented fix is simply room temperature for at least 30 minutes — see Philips Error 11 or 19.

Garage deliveries and cold rooms

The trap is timing. A machine off a delivery van feels fine to the touch within the hour, but the thermoblock — a dense block of metal — lags far behind the panels around it. Philips allows 30 minutes at room temperature; Jura repair practice gives a cold machine a full hour, and a large super-automatic can take several hours to genuinely reach room temperature. The failure pattern is always the same: the machine worked before, the code appears at the first start-up, and nothing you press clears it.

Most at risk: winter deliveries, and machines kept in conservatories, garage offices, utility rooms and holiday homes that sit unheated between visits. If a machine must live somewhere cold, bring it into a heated room well before you plan to use it — not while you are standing there waiting for a coffee.

Condensation: the risk nobody mentions

Temperature is only half of the problem. Move a cold machine into a warm, humid kitchen and moisture condenses onto every cold surface inside it — boiler, wiring, connectors, control board. Powering a damp machine is the real hazard of winter set-ups: the lockout codes are protective, but electricity and condensation do not mix.

So the warm-up has two phases. Let the machine equalise to room temperature, then let any condensation dry — which takes longer than the machine takes to stop feeling cold. If you can see or suspect moisture, leave it unplugged until fully dry. Never plug a cold machine in "to warm it up faster" — that is the one move that can turn a benign lockout into a real fault.

A safe warm-up protocol

  1. Bring the machine into a heated room and leave it unplugged.
  2. Give it real time: at least 30 minutes for a Philips or Saeco, an hour minimum for a Jura, and several hours if the chassis has been deeply cold or the machine is large.
  3. Inspect for condensation. If there is any sign of moisture, wait until it has dried completely before going further.
  4. On a Jura you can help it along gently: fill the tank with warm — not hot — tap water, or run a hair dryer on low into the water-tank cavity for five minutes. Never hotter, and never aimed at the body or electronics.
  5. Power on and let the start-up sequence finish, then run a rinse or a cup of hot water.
  6. If the code has cleared, nothing was broken — keep the machine somewhere warmer. If it persists on a genuinely warm machine, the sensor circuit is the fault.

When warmth does not fix it

A code that survives a proper warm-up is real. On a Jura, a genuine Error 2 means the NTC sensor or the thermal-fuse cords on the coffee thermoblock have failed; technicians replace both together, and the parts run about €25 to €40 for the sensor and €15 to €30 for the fuse cords — under €50, usually worth it. On a Philips or Saeco, a cold-type code that recurs on a warm machine means a failed temperature sensor: a service repair with a part in the €20 to €40 range. Either way, warm the machine fully before ordering anything.

After storage: not every code is the cold

A machine waking from months in storage can show faults unrelated to temperature. A De'Longhi that greets you with General Alarm is complaining about its infuser nine times out of ten — stuck, dirty or out of position — and the fix is rinsing the infuser, not warming the machine; see General Alarm, codes 1101 and 1512.
