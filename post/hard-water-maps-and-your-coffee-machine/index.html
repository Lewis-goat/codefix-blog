Hard water maps and your coffee machine

2026-11-13

Hardness is the single biggest variable in how long a coffee machine's heating system survives, and it is invisible until something goes wrong. Utilities across Europe publish hard water maps showing regional averages, and they are a useful starting point. But your machine does not drink the regional average — it drinks what comes out of your tap today. Here is how to translate the map into a maintenance strategy your machine actually benefits from.

What hardness bands actually mean

Hardness is mostly dissolved calcium and magnesium, reported in three common units: ppm (milligrams per litre as calcium carbonate), German degrees (°dH) and French degrees (°fH). The conversions are fixed — one German degree is about 17.8 ppm and one French degree is exactly 10 ppm — so any map, test strip or machine menu can be compared on a single scale. The bands most maps and manufacturers use:

  • Soft, below 60 ppm (under 3.4 °dH). Scale forms slowly; descaling once or twice a year is usually enough.
  • Moderately hard, 60–120 ppm (3.4–6.7 °dH). A kettle shows a visible crust within weeks; machines need a routine.
  • Hard, 120–180 ppm (6.7–10.1 °dH). Scale grows fast enough to shorten descale intervals noticeably.
  • Very hard, above 180 ppm (over 10.1 °dH). Limescale, not mechanical wear, becomes the main lifetime limiter for the heater.

Two neighbouring towns can sit in different bands, because maps generalise across supply zones. Use the map to set expectations, then confirm at the tap.

How carbonate scale forms in a thermoblock

The scale in coffee machines is calcium carbonate — chemically the same as chalk. Rain dissolves calcium as it percolates through rock, carrying it to your tap as dissolved calcium bicarbonate. Heating breaks that compound apart: carbon dioxide is driven off, and the calcium left behind combines with carbonate and falls out of solution. It deposits wherever water is hottest and moves slowest, which in a coffee machine means the heating system and the finest jets.

A thermoblock concentrates the process. Water is pumped through a narrow milled channel in an aluminium or stainless block that runs deliberately hot, so a thin film of water touches very hot metal. The first grains stick there, and chalk insulates far better than the metal it coats, so the block must run hotter to deliver the same water temperature — which accelerates the next round of deposition. That feedback loop is why a scaled thermoblock goes from fine to failing within a season rather than fading over years. The early symptoms are longer heat-up, weaker steam and slower flow; ignored, scale upsets the temperature readings, trips overheat protection or blocks the channel.

Setting your machine's hardness level correctly

Most bean-to-cup machines from Jura and Philips Saeco, and their De'Longhi equivalents, include a water hardness setting. It does not soften anything — it tells the machine which descale reminder schedule to run. Set it too low and the machine waits too long between descalings while scale builds.

  1. Run the cold tap for a few seconds and fill a clean glass with freshly drawn water.
  2. Dip a hardness test strip for the time stated on the pack — strips cost about €5–€10 for a pack of fifty.
  3. Compare the colour fields to the chart and note your band in °dH or ppm.
  4. Open the machine's settings menu and set water hardness to the matching level.
  5. Re-test once a year, or after any change to your supply, such as a new building softener or a house move.

Check the manual before combining a filter with this setting: on several models, fitting a filter is meant to go together with the softest hardness setting, because the machine then tracks filter life instead of counting litres toward scale. Jura's own documentation states which combinations its machines expect.

Filter or descale: choosing a strategy

  • The filter route softens water at the machine by ion exchange. Cartridges cost roughly €15–€30 and their capacity is rated in litres, not months — Philips publishes the figure for each of its filters, and the machine counts down from it.
  • The descale route costs a bottle of citric or lactic acid descaler at €8–€15, used every four to eight weeks in hard water. Cheaper per year, but the cycle must be run on time — ignored reminders leave the De'Longhi Magnifica Dinamica's descale light staying on while the scale keeps growing.
  • Match the strategy to your band. Soft and moderately hard water: descaling on schedule is usually enough. Hard and very hard water: a filter is cheaper than a heating assembly.
  • Mixed strategies fail quietly. A cartridge run past its litre capacity lets full hardness back in while the machine still believes it is protected.

Capsule machines signal the same problem differently — Nespresso Vertuo models use light patterns rather than codes — but the chemistry is identical.

When not to trust the map

Maps generalise; private wells, rooftop tanks and building softeners all change what reaches your machine. The most reliable test is free: open your kettle. A chalky element means your tap is hard whatever the map says, and a clean element after months of use means your descale intervals can relax. Pair that with a yearly strip test and your hardness setting will track reality rather than geography.
