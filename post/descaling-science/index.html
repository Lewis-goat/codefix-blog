Descaling, explained properly

2026-10-25

Every coffee machine fault list has one root cause that dominates: limescale. Hard water carries dissolved calcium and magnesium; heat that water and the carbonates drop out of solution, plating onto the hottest metal in the machine. That is all scale is, and it sits behind an outsized share of the codes on this site — heater faults, valve faults, priming failures and stuck descale lights among them. It is also the only fault family with a €10 fix.

What scale does to a thermoblock

A thermoblock is a mass of metal with a narrow channel through which water is heated on its way to the cup. Scale plates onto those channel walls, and calcium carbonate is a decent thermal insulator — which is exactly the problem.

  • Heating slows down. The element runs longer to reach target temperature, and heavy scale slows heating enough to trip the board's own temperature checks on some machines — Jura's thermoblock error family explicitly ties scale to these codes.
  • The sensor lies. A layer of scale changes how quickly the temperature sensor in the block sees heat, so the board can believe the machine is overheating, or failing to heat, when neither is true.
  • The block runs hot. Insulated metal sits above its design temperature, which stresses the thermal fuses wired to protect it. A scaled-solid thermoblock is a replace-it part, not a clean-it part — €90 to €180 on a Jura, for scale a bottle of descaler would have removed.

What scale does to valves and small orifices

The second damage site is everything narrow. On Jura machines with the motor-driven ceramic valve, scale stiffens the disc until it cannot reach the position the board commanded — that is literally what Error 6 reports, and why a full descale is the first, free step in its fix. Narrow inlets clog the same way: Philips Error 05 can persist after priming because a scaled inlet will not let the pump draw water at all. Level sensors are fair game too — scale on a tank float is a classic cause of a false "fill water tank" message on a full tank. And a scaled flow sensor stops counting, which is one way a De'Longhi descale light stays on after descaling: the machine never registered the cycle you ran.

Why vinegar is wrong for some machines

Household vinegar is acetic acid, and for espresso machines it has three strikes. It is weak, so it needs long contact to soften compacted scale inside a compact channel. It attacks chrome-plated brass fittings and swells some rubber seals. And its smell survives many rinses inside plastic tubing. Nespresso is explicit about the consequence — do not descale with vinegar, it damages the circuit and voids help — which is worth remembering when a Vertuo's orange blinking pattern sends you to the descale routine. A kettle? Vinegar is fine. A machine with a pump, a valve and a warranty? Use a proper descaler.

Citric vs lactic acid

Almost every commercial descaler is one of two food-safe acids:

  • Citric acid — sold as powder, €3 to €6 for enough to last most households a year, strong and fast on heavy scale. It is the base of most universal descalers; rinsed well, it leaves nothing behind.
  • Lactic acid — milder and nearly odourless, common in the branded bottles machines ship with. Gentler on seals and plating, at the cost of needing a slightly longer contact time to shift the same scale.

Either works when run through the machine's own descale programme. What matters is completing it: reminder counters only register a full cycle run from the descale menu, spout fitted, rinse phase included. Stop early or freestyle it with a jug, and the light stays on.

How often: it depends on your water

Machine reminders are usually volume-based — cups poured, litres pumped — not hardness-based, so hard-water households need to descale before the light ever comes on. A practical rule of thumb:

  • Soft water (below about 7 °dH, or 125 ppm): the machine's own reminder is enough, in practice every 3 to 6 months.
  • Moderately hard (7 to 14 °dH): every 2 to 3 months.
  • Hard (above 14 °dH): roughly monthly, and earlier if the flow slows or the pump gets loud.

A pack of hardness test strips costs €5 to €10, and your water supplier publishes the figure for your area. The late signs are consistent across brands: slow, skinny flows, a louder pump, cooler coffee — and eventually the codes above. Descale on schedule and most of those codes never happen; a €10 bottle is the cheapest repair this site will ever recommend.
