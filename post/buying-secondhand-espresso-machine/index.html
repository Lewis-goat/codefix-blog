Buying a secondhand super-automatic: the error-code test drive

2026-10-29

A secondhand super-automatic is either the best deal in coffee or a donation to someone's kitchen clear-out, and the difference is usually visible on the display. These machines check themselves at start-up and put their faults on screen as codes, which makes the display the most honest participant in the sale. Before money changes hands, make reading those codes the centrepiece of your test drive.

Ask the seller to run it

The machine is plugged in at their house, not yours, so the test happens there. Ask for this sequence and be present for all of it:

  1. A true cold start. Have the machine switched off, not standing by. On a Jura the brew unit runs a full cycle at start-up; listen for stalling, grinding or a long silence.
  2. One drink from beans. Grind, brew, eject the puck, and watch the display the whole time.
  3. Hot water and steam, if the model has them.
  4. A rinse or cleaning cycle. A Jura cleaning programme takes about fifteen minutes; if nobody will wait that long, insist on a rinse at minimum.
  5. Photograph every code exactly as shown. Codes vanish on reset, and the photo is your record and your negotiating note.

One honest caveat: a machine fetched from a cold garage can throw a temperature-lockout code that is not a fault — Jura's Error 2 appears when the machine is below about 10 °C, and Philips machines have an equivalent too-cold code. Give it half an hour at room temperature and retest; a code that persists warm is real.

Codes that are cheap fixes

None of these should end a sale. At most they are talking points on price:

  • Jura Error 8 — the brew group did not complete its cycle. Most cases clear with a cleaning programme and a €15 to €25 box of tablets; the worst case is a brew group at €80 to €150. The Error 8 page has the full routine.
  • Philips and Saeco Error 20 — brew unit or service door. The documented reset is to refit the drip tray and grounds container, close the service door and switch off and on; a rinse plus a little silicone grease on the brew group clears most cases. Details on the Error 20 page.
  • De'Longhi General Alarm — nine times out of ten the infuser wants a rinse under the tap and a correct refit; the assembly is €35 to €60 if it is genuinely damaged.
  • Blockage and priming codes — Saeco's blocked-funnel and air-in-circuit errors, or a De'Longhi water-circuit message, are spoon-and-water fixes that cost nothing.

The removable brew group is your window

On Philips/Saeco and De'Longhi machines the brew group or infuser slides out through the service door. Ask to remove it. A group that is lightly soiled versus caked in old coffee oils tells you how the machine was actually kept — and a replacement is a €35 to €80 part, not a write-off.

Codes that mean a price cut — or a walk-away

  • Jura Error 7 — the valve or brew-group drive did not reach its commanded position. It has no reliable user-level fix; the valve assembly is €60 to €150 and service labour typically exceeds the part, with GIGA multi-valves documented as a Jura service job. Our Error 7 breakdown has the detail.
  • Jura thermoblock codes, Errors 1 to 5 — the sensors are cheap and thermal-fuse cords run €15 to €30, but a thermoblock is €90 to €180 and it all sits behind security screws at mains voltage. A recurring heating-failure code on an old machine is a quote-first situation.
  • Philips internal-fault codes, 02, 10, 15 and 22 — unpublished and routed to service. A board is €80 to €150. On a LatteGo or Xelsis a repair can still make sense; on a ten-year-old Saeco, price a replacement instead.
  • De'Longhi leak alarm — water detected in the base, where the power board lives. Walk away unless the price is low enough to fund a full seal-and-hose overhaul.
  • Miele F77 — an internal valve-initialisation fault whose entire user remedy is a power cycle. If it recurs during your test, price in service: a valve runs €50 to €120 and the control board more. The F77 page explains the diagnosis.

Brand notes for the viewing

Jura uses the same numbered errors across its range, so the code you photograph identifies the fault family whatever the model. De'Longhi machines mostly show words rather than numbers, though newer models also log the numeric codes that service uses — ask the seller to bring them up. Philips publishes six user-fixable Saeco codes and routes the rest to service, so anything outside the friendly six is a repairer conversation before you buy.

Finally, do the arithmetic out loud: the asking price plus the worst-case repair from the codes you photographed, set against a refurbished unit. Manufacturer service on a super-automatic runs €250 to €500 including return shipping, which is a fair benchmark for negotiation. And if the seller will not run the machine while you watch, that is your answer — walk away.
