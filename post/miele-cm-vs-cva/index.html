Miele CM vs CVA: same codes, different machines

2026-11-07

Miele builds its coffee systems in two very different packages. The CM line — the CM 5510 and CM 6150 — is a countertop machine: it plugs into a wall socket and fills from a removable water container. The CVA line — the CVA 6401 and CVA 6805 — is built into kitchen cabinetry, and these installations are often plumbed into the mains supply rather than filled by hand. Outwardly they could not look less alike; inside, they are close enough that Miele runs the same built-in self-diagnosis in both, and both report faults as F-codes.

That shared table is why an F-code means the same on a countertop CM as on a built-in CVA. What the line does change is which cause is most likely and how much of the repair you can do yourself.

One fault table, two manuals

Miele prints its fault meanings in the operating instructions for each line, and the wording varies slightly between the CM countertop manuals and the CVA built-in manuals. The self-diagnosis behind the code is the same; the sentences describing it are not. So if you search your manual's exact phrase and land on advice for the other line, keep reading — a wording gap, not a different fault, is why the search only nearly matched.

Both manuals draw the same line around self-help. Miele states that the outer casing must not be opened: the machines carry internal voltages and a pressurised water system. Across the F-table the split is consistent — the water-supply codes are the user-fixable ones; the valve and brew-unit codes usually need Miele service. That split holds for both CM and CVA owners.

F10 and F17: the water fault both lines share

F10 and F17 share one manual entry: the machine tried to draw water in and failed, or managed only a trickle. It is the friendliest fault in the Miele table, because the manual fix is usually the whole fix. What differs between the lines is where the water failed to come from.

On a countertop CM

The cause is almost always the removable container: it is empty, it is not seated on its valve, or the valve itself is sticking. You can check all three without tools.

On a plumbed-in CVA

The suspicion moves upstream, to the shut-off valve in the supply line and the inline filter. The container is not the suspect if there is no container. The sequence either way is short:

  1. Remove the water container, fill it with fresh cold tap water and reinsert it until it locks.
  2. Check the container's valve seat for a stuck or dirty seal and rinse it under running water.
  3. On plumbed machines, confirm the shut-off valve is fully open and the inline filter is not clogged.
  4. If the fault recurs intermittently, descale the water intake — scale in the valve makes the fault come and go.
  5. A machine that still reports the fault with a verified supply needs its intake valve or pump looked at by Miele service.

In cost terms this fault is usually free; a failed intake valve runs about €30 to €60. The F10 and F17 page walks through the whole sequence.

F77: the internal fault both lines fear

F77 is Miele's catch-all for an internal malfunction detected at start-up — in practice centred on the valve system failing to initialise. The manual remedy is honest about the limits of self-help:

  1. Switch the machine off with the On/Off sensor, then unplug it from the wall.
  2. Leave it off for several minutes; if F77 came back after a brief off-period before, give it up to an hour.
  3. Plug back in and power on, noting whether the fault appears immediately at initialisation or only when a drink is requested.

F77 that returns after the power cycle is a component failure — valve, pump or control board — and belongs with Miele service. Note the model number when you call: the CM 5510/6150 and CVA 6401/6805 differ internally, so the same code does not promise the same failed part. A valve runs €50 to €120 with the control board dearer. The F77 page covers what to record before you phone.

Countertop versus built-in, in practice

  • A CM can be unplugged and moved to clear counter space; a CVA is a fixed installation, so isolate the power before doing anything at the machine.
  • A CM's water path starts at a container you hold in your hand; a CVA's starts at the plumbing, so a "no water" fault has a longer chain to check — and a leak has a cupboard underneath it.
  • Out-of-warranty manufacturer service on a super-automatic typically lands in the €250 to €500 range including return shipping; a built-in unit has to be freed from its housing before anyone can quote, which is one more reason to catch water-supply faults early on that line.
  • Independent espresso repairers are usually cheaper than the manufacturer route for a single known part — but Miele's warning about opening the casing applies to whoever opens it.
