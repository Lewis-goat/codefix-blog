O-rings and gaskets: the ten-euro fix behind many leaks

2026-11-12

A puddle under a bean-to-cup machine rarely means the machine is finished. In a large share of workshop leak repairs, the failed part is a small rubber seal that costs less than a coffee on a terrace. Knowing where O-rings and gaskets live, and what their failure looks like from the outside, turns an intimidating fault into a ten-euro job.

Where O-rings and gaskets actually live

Under the water tank

Nearly every removable tank seals through a spring-loaded outlet valve with one or two small O-rings. When the tank sits down on the machine, its weight presses the valve open against these rings. They harden with age and scale, and the classic symptom is precise: a puddle that appears only when the tank is seated, even with the machine switched off.

On the brew unit

Bean-to-cup machines push water through the coffee under pressure, and the moving piston in the brew group runs on seals — visible on machines with a removable infuser, hidden inside on machines with a fixed brew group. Dried coffee oil is their main enemy, which is why rinsing the infuser regularly, as DeLonghi's own care guidance recommends, keeps seals supple far longer than cleaning tablets alone (DeLonghi care instructions).

At the heater unions

Where the thermoblock or boiler meets pipes and hoses you find compression fittings with small gaskets, plus clamped silicone hoses. A weeping union rarely announces itself as a drip: look for a faint scale streak or crusty white trace running down from a fitting, because hot water often evaporates before it falls.

Around the pump and valve block

The vibration pump sits on rubber grommets, and solenoid valves carry tiny gaskets. A leak here tends to appear only while the pump is running, which is a useful clue in itself.

Symptoms that point to a seal

  • A puddle that appears only with the tank seated points at the tank valve rings.
  • A drip tray that fills much faster than it used to signals a slow internal weep above it.
  • A visible water line or scale streak inside the casing should be traced upward to its fitting.
  • A machine that struggles to prime, or reports an empty water circuit while the tank is full, may be drawing air through a worn seal — DeLonghi's fill-circuit message is a classic example.
  • Leak-sensing models raise a code outright: DeLonghi's leak alarm 3963 is the machine telling you exactly what this article is about.

Before ordering anything, run the cheapest diagnostic there is: dry the whole machine, lay strips of paper towel along the suspect zones, run one rinse cycle, and see which strip wets first. Then check the old seals with a fingernail — a healthy O-ring springs back round, while an aged one keeps the flattened shape it was compressed into. If it stays oval, it is done.

Doing the swap

  1. Unplug the machine and let it cool completely.
  2. Photograph each area before you remove anything, including hose routings.
  3. Work on one seal at a time so you always know where the next one came from.
  4. Match by size, not by appearance: O-rings are specified by inner diameter and cord thickness in millimetres, and a ring that is "close enough" in cord thickness will weep.
  5. Use food-safe silicone or EPDM rated for hot drinking water — never unlabelled bulk rings of unknown elastomer.
  6. Lubricate the new seal with food-safe silicone grease; a small tube costs €5–€8 and lasts years. Never use cooking oil, and avoid petroleum jelly, which can swell some rubbers.
  7. Leak-test before you call it done: reassemble, run a rinse over fresh paper towels, and check again after an hour.

What you should pay

Individual O-rings typically run €0.50–€3 each, and brand-specific seal kits land in the €8–€15 range. That is the entire cost of the "ten-euro fix", and it is why a leak deserves one honest Saturday-morning attempt before anyone talks about replacing the machine. Community teardowns such as those on iFixit show disassembly photos that reveal where seals sit in common models.

When it is not the seal

Cracked tanks, split hoses, damaged quick-connectors and loose clamps cause the same symptoms, and no O-ring will fix them. If a leak survives a full seal swap, the machine needs a bench pressure test — start from our Jura hub or DeLonghi hub for model-specific next steps. Leak codes are not unique to coffee machines, either: dishwashers flag them too, as with GE's E1 leak code. But in an appliance that heats and pumps water every day, the humble O-ring remains the first suspect worth clearing — cheaply, and usually rightly.
